A short guide to complexity theory

Between P and NP-complete.

Could some problems in NP be neither efficiently solvable nor as hard as every problem in NP? Meet the NP-intermediate problems—and the theorem that says they exist if P and NP are different.

01 / The setup

Three ideas to begin.

Think of these classes as ways to group decision problems by how efficiently they can be solved or checked.

P

Efficiently solvable

P contains decision problems that a deterministic algorithm can solve in polynomial time.

NP

Efficiently checkable

For problems in NP, a proposed yes-answer has a certificate that can be checked in polynomial time. P is contained in NP.

P ≟ NP

The open question

We do not know whether P = NP. If they are equal, every NP problem is efficiently solvable; if not, some are not.

02 / The key result

Ladner's theorem

If P ≠ NP, then there exists a language in NP that is neither in P nor NP-complete (under polynomial-time many-one reductions).

Why it matters: NP is not forced to split neatly into just easy problems and NP-complete problems. Under the assumption P ≠ NP, at least one problem lies in between—an NP-intermediate problem, or NPI.

Core intuition: The proof constructs a deliberately artificial language. A controlled, gradually increasing amount of computational work keeps it outside P, while diagonalization against candidate reductions prevents it from being NP-complete. The construction depends on P ≠ NP; it does not identify a familiar, natural problem as NPI.

03 / Natural candidates

Problems that might fit.

These are often discussed as possible NP-intermediate problems. Their exact complexity status remains unknown.

Graph isomorphism

Given two graphs, decide whether a relabeling of one makes it identical to the other. A proposed relabeling is easy to check, so the problem is in NP. It has a quasipolynomial-time algorithm, but no polynomial-time algorithm is known.

NPI candidate

Integer factorization

For a decision version, ask whether an integer N has a divisor d with 1 < d ≤ k. The divisor is a polynomial-size certificate, so this problem is in NP. No classical polynomial-time factoring algorithm is known, and this decision problem is not known to be NP-complete.

NPI candidate

Important: Neither problem is proven to be NP-intermediate. P ≠ NP guarantees that some NPI language exists, but does not tell us whether either of these natural candidates qualifies.
